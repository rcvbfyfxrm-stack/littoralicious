FlavorDB: Compound-Level Flavor Pairing

A peer-reviewed open-access database (Garg et al., Nucleic Acids Research, 2018) maps 25,595 flavor molecules across 936 ingredients. It documents the shared volatile compounds behind strawberry-and-basil, chocolate-and-blue-cheese, tomato-and-clam. Here is how to read it — and where the food-pairing hypothesis breaks down.

The Science Behind Pairing

Most cooks build pairings from tradition or palate memory. The food-pairing hypothesis offers a third route: a chemical one. The proposal — formalised by Ahn, Ahnert, Bagrow and Barabási in Scientific Reports (2011, n=56,498 recipes across 11 cuisines) — states that ingredients sharing more volatile compounds are more likely to taste harmonious together. Western cuisines in their dataset showed the predicted pattern; East Asian cuisines showed the opposite.

The hypothesis grew out of the workshop conversation in 2002 between Heston Blumenthal and Sosa Ingredients flavour scientist François Benzi, who noticed that white chocolate and caviar share trimethylamine and other amines. Until 2018, working with shared compounds meant access to industry flavour databases, paywalled journals, or consultancies. That changed when IIIT-Delhi published FlavorDB.

Ahn et al. (Scientific Reports 1:196, 2011) tested the food-pairing hypothesis on 56,498 recipes across 11 regional cuisines. North American and Western European cuisines preferred ingredient pairs sharing many volatile compounds. East Asian and Southern European cuisines preferred pairs sharing few. The hypothesis is regional, not universal.

Not anymore. FlavorDB is a free, open-access database built by researchers at IIIT-Delhi that maps the complete flavor molecule profile of 936 natural ingredients. Every compound. Every shared molecule. Every unexpected connection.

What FlavorDB Actually Is

FlavorDB is a searchable database of flavor molecules — the chemical compounds responsible for how food tastes and smells. It was built by the Computational Social Lab at IIIT-Delhi and published as open research.

Here is what makes it useful: every ingredient in the database is broken down into its constituent flavor molecules, and the database can show you which ingredients share molecules. That is the testable claim at the centre of food-pairing theory — ingredients that share volatile compounds are more likely to taste harmonious together, in cuisines where the pattern holds.

FlavorDB contains 2,254 naturally occurring flavor molecules and 13,869 synthetic compounds. For cooking, focus on the natural molecules — they map the volatile profiles of real ingredients.

The database contains two types of molecules:

  • 2,254 naturally occurring molecules — the compounds found in real ingredients (fruits, meats, spices, dairy, vegetables)
  • 13,869 synthetic compounds — used in flavoring industry and fragrance. Less relevant for cooking, but fascinating for understanding how food manufacturers replicate natural flavors

How Flavor Compounds Work

Before reading the database, understand what is in it. Every food contains volatile organic compounds. Strawberry has roughly 360 identified volatiles (Schwieterman et al., PLoS ONE, 2014, n=35 cultivars); only a handful are odour-active at typical concentrations. Your brain does not process them individually — it reads the pattern as “strawberry.”

Schwieterman et al. (PLoS ONE 9:e88446, 2014) identified ~360 volatile compounds in strawberry across 35 cultivars; only ~20 are consistently odour-active. Olfaction uses ~400 functional human receptor genes (Niimura, 2014, Genome Biology and Evolution); gustation, five basic-taste channels. Flavor lives in the olfactory channel.

The Two Systems

  • Gustatory (taste): The tongue detects five basic tastes — sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami — through approximately 25 functional taste-receptor genes (Bachmanov & Beauchamp, Annual Review of Nutrition, 2007). Low-resolution information: is it sweet? How sweet?
  • Olfactory (smell): The nose and retronasal passage detect volatile compounds through approximately 400 functional olfactory receptor genes in humans (Niimura, Genome Biology and Evolution, 2014). This is where flavor lives. It is why food registers as bland during a cold — the olfactory channel is occluded. The difference between a strawberry and a raspberry is almost entirely olfactory, not gustatory.

Flavor pairing theory works on the olfactory channel. The hypothesis: when two ingredients share key volatile compounds, your brain perceives harmony. The shared molecules create a bridge — a chemical common ground that makes the combination feel "right."

Why Some Pairings Feel Inevitable

Tomato and basil read as inevitable to anyone raised on Italian food. The chemistry confirms what the tradition encoded: both contain linalool (a terpene alcohol with floral, slightly spicy character) and several shared esters and aldehydes. The grandmother who first combined them in a Campanian kitchen had no compound list. Her palate ran the experiment.

Chocolate and blue cheese is the inverse case — no shared tradition, but FlavorDB shows overlap in methylketones and lactones generated during fermentation and ageing. The pairing was popularised by Heston Blumenthal’s Fat Duck kitchen after the 2002 Sosa workshop with François Benzi — one of the early documented uses of compound-overlap reasoning in restaurant cooking.

How to Use FlavorDB: A Practical Guide

Before You Start

Go to cosylab.iiitd.edu.in/flavordb. Free, no account, works on a phone. The interface is academic because it was built for researchers; the underlying data is the same that flavour scientists work with.

Method 1: Start with an Ingredient

You have a hero ingredient — say, fresh figs. You want to know what pairs with it beyond the obvious (prosciutto, goat cheese). Here's the workflow:

  1. Search for your ingredient — type "fig" in the entity search. The database recognises synonyms, so "aubergine" finds the same entry as "eggplant."
  2. View its flavor molecule profile — the database lists every known volatile compound in that ingredient, with their flavor descriptions (fruity, woody, floral, etc.)
  3. Use Flavor Pairing — this is the power feature. Click "Flavor Pairing" and the database shows you every other ingredient that shares molecules with fig, ranked by the number of shared compounds.
  4. Read the results — you'll see categories first (fruit, spice, dairy), then specific ingredients with shared molecule counts. The higher the count, the stronger the molecular bridge.

The fig search surfaces partners that may not be obvious: chili peppers sharing fruity esters, black tea sharing aromatic compounds, lamb sharing fatty-acid derivatives. These are candidates — hypotheses to taste, not guarantees of harmony.

Use the Flavor Pairing feature to generate candidate partners. A higher shared-compound count is a stronger hypothesis, not a proof — remember Ahn et al. (2011) found East Asian and Southern European cuisines reward fewer shared compounds, not more. Look for cross-category matches, then taste before plating.

Method 2: Start with a Molecule

You know you want "something with a roasted, nutty character" in your dish. Search by flavor profile:

  1. Go to Molecular Search
  2. Filter by flavor profile: "nutty," "roasted," "caramel"
  3. See which molecules produce that flavor — pyrazines for roasted/nutty, furanones for caramel
  4. Then check which ingredients contain those molecules

Result: the “roasted” character in coffee, sesame, and bread crust traces to the same family of alkylpyrazines, products of the Maillard reaction. Sesame-crusted tuna with a coffee-infused jus is a chemistry-driven hypothesis worth testing — the volatile profiles converge.

Method 3: Explore the Flavor Network

FlavorDB includes an interactive network graph showing connections between all 936 ingredients based on shared flavor molecules. Ingredients that share four or more molecules are connected. It's visually dense, but the patterns are revealing:

  • Fruits and dairy cluster together — large overlapping families of esters and lactones. Fruit-and-cheese pairings recur across many European traditions.
  • Spices form a dense hub — most spices share terpene compounds with dozens of other ingredients. They function as molecular bridges across categories.
  • Aged meats and fermented foods overlap — via shared amino-acid derivatives and Maillard products. This is part of why soy sauce or anchovy reinforces, rather than contradicts, the savoury profile of beef.

Practical Applications for Yacht Chefs

The Substitution Problem

You're in a Caribbean port. The recipe calls for yuzu. There is no yuzu. FlavorDB tells you which locally available citrus shares the most volatile compounds with yuzu. Maybe it's a specific lime variety with a particular limonene-to-citral ratio. You're not guessing — you're making an informed substitution based on molecular similarity.

The Dietary Restriction Puzzle

Guest is allergic to shellfish. Menu was built around a langoustine bisque. You need a substitute carrying the briny, sweet, savoury register. FlavorDB shows that certain seaweeds and fermented mushrooms share volatile compounds with crustaceans, including dimethyl sulfide and specific C8 aldehydes. The “lobster bisque without lobster” is not a compromise; it is a substitution informed by overlapping volatile chemistry. Test before service.

Cross-Category Combinations

To build pairings that read as unexpected, search for ingredients sharing compounds across very different food categories. Strawberry and black pepper share rotundone and several terpenoids; pineapple and blue cheese share methylketones and short-chain esters; coffee and grapefruit share furanones and pyrazines. The chemistry gives a defensible reason for the plate.

Wine Pairing

Wine grapes are indexed in FlavorDB. The signature volatiles of Sauvignon Blanc — 3-mercaptohexan-1-ol, 4-methyl-4-mercaptopentan-2-one, methoxypyrazines (documented by Tominaga et al., Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, 1998) — map directly onto compounds in green herbs, citrus zest, and smoked seafood. Pairing on shared volatiles is one tool among several; acid, tannin, and weight still have to be balanced.

The Limits of Molecular Pairing

What this does not mean: FlavorDB does not tell you a dish will work. The food-pairing hypothesis is regionally contingent (Ahn et al., 2011) and a 2014 reanalysis by Varshney et al. (Bulletin of Mathematical Biology) showed the original effect attenuates once recipe-frequency biases are controlled. Treat shared compounds as a hypothesis generator, not a verdict.

FlavorDB is a tool, not an oracle. Shared molecules describe potential harmony; they do not guarantee a finished dish. The database does not capture:

  • Concentration matters. Two ingredients might share a compound, but if it's present at 0.001% in one and 15% in the other, the pairing might not work as expected. FlavorDB tells you the compound exists — not how much of it is there.
  • Cooking transforms molecules. Raw garlic and roasted garlic have completely different volatile profiles. The Maillard reaction creates new compounds that don't exist in raw ingredients. The database captures raw profiles primarily.
  • Texture and temperature. A pairing that works as a cold salad might fail as a hot soup. The physical experience of eating — crunch, creaminess, temperature contrast — is invisible to molecular analysis.
  • Cultural context. Your brain's experience of "pleasant" is shaped by what you grew up eating. A pairing that FlavorDB predicts as harmonious might feel wrong to a guest whose food culture has no reference for it.
  • Interaction effects. Molecules don't always combine linearly. Two pleasant compounds can create an unpleasant third interaction. Chemistry is more complex than addition.

Use FlavorDB as a starting point for exploration, not as a replacement for tasting. The database generates hypotheses. Your palate tests them.

FlavorDB maps raw ingredient profiles. Cooking transforms molecules: raw garlic and roasted garlic have completely different volatile profiles. Shared compounds also vary in concentration — a match at 0.001% in one ingredient and 15% in another may not produce the harmony you expect. Always taste.

How to Build a Dish Using FlavorDB

A practical workflow for creating a new dish from molecular principles:

The Molecular Mise en Place

  1. Choose your hero. One ingredient you want to build around. Search it in FlavorDB. Note its top 5-10 flavor molecules and their descriptions (fruity, woody, floral, etc.).
  2. Find the bridges. Run the Flavor Pairing search. Identify 3-4 unexpected ingredients that share the most molecules with your hero — ideally from different food categories.
  3. Check the flavor descriptions. Don't just count shared molecules — read what they taste like. If your hero and the pairing partner share "green, grassy" compounds but you want a warm dish, that bridge might not serve you.
  4. Build the composition. Your hero + one molecular bridge ingredient + one textural contrast + one acid or aromatic to lift. Classic composition, molecular justification.
  5. Test in small batches. Make a tiny portion. Taste. The molecule said it would work — does your palate agree? Adjust.
  6. Name it. If it works, you've created something with a story: "These ingredients share [compound]. That's why they work." Guests love the science. It's a conversation, not just a plate.

The Bigger Picture: Why This Matters

Every great culinary tradition is, at its molecular core, a set of flavor pairing discoveries made through centuries of trial and error. Thai cuisine figured out that fish sauce (glutamates, amines) bridges lime (citral, limonene) and chili (capsaicin, dihydrocapsaicin) through shared volatile pathways. French cuisine discovered that butter (diacetyl, lactones) bridges nearly everything because its molecular profile is a universal solvent for flavors.

FlavorDB doesn't replace tradition. It explains it. And in explaining it, it opens doors that tradition alone never could — because tradition is limited by geography, culture, and what ingredients happened to grow near each other. Molecular analysis has no borders.

The cook who reads both — the grandmother’s tradition and the molecule’s logic — has more tools at the pass. The plate still has to land.

“FlavorDB does not replace tradition. It documents the shared compounds behind some of it, and proposes candidates outside it. Tradition is bounded by geography and what grew nearby; molecular analysis is bounded only by the limits of the data set.”
